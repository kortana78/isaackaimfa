On "There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom"

13 July 2026

I am a big fan of nanotech, nanobots, molecular machines, and everything at the nanoscale. I'm also a fan of Feynmanology (It's a doctrine lol). I had previously watched his lecture There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom, but over time my perspective on its central idea has changed, so I decided to read the original text instead of relying on videos.

Feynman begins by imagining shrinking physical objects and reducing the space they occupy. One of his examples is compressing the entire Encyclopædia Britannica into an incredibly tiny area. Reading this today made me wonder what he would think of the device I'm using to write these thoughts, a device that can store an entire library in the palm of my hand. In that sense, his specific example feels less striking today than it must have in 1959.

Still, I think there are things where miniaturization could transform society far more than simply storing books. Tiny batteries, microscopic sensors, medical nanobots, and other nanoscale machines seem much more exciting to me than compressed encyclopedias. I'm optimistic about technology, sometimes optimistic enough to imagine that humans might one day exist at dramatically smaller scales. It's not something I usually say out loud because most people around me don't share that kind of science fiction enthusiasm.

As the lecture progresses, Feynman turns to computers, and this is where I think many of his ideas have aged remarkably well. His discussion goes beyond simply making things smaller. He points toward engineering problems and possibilities that I hadn't considered when I first thought about nanotechnology. Some of those ideas also seem absent from many popular discussions of nanotech today although perhaps researchers are thinking about them and I'm simply unaware of it.

Overall, I think There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom is an excellent example of how tech predictions can be simultaneously inaccurate and profoundly useful. Many of Feynman's specific expectations did not unfold exactly as he imagined, yet the questions he asked helped shape entire fields of research. Nanotechnology is still evolving, and I hope to look back at this reflection decades from now and see which of my own expectations turned out to be wrong and which captured something important. I suspect the same will be true for artificial intelligence, and perhaps for every emerging technology.

Our predictions may miss the details, but they can still point us toward the future.
