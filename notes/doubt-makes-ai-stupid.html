Doubt Makes AI Stupid

20 October 2026

I found a version of the idea that “hard work beats talent when talent doesn’t work hard,” but one that applies to large language models. I found it the hard way: it turns out that more advanced models are not always better at solving hard problems.

Bertrand Russell said, and I quote, “The fundamental cause of the trouble is that in the modern world the stupid are cocksure while the intelligent are full of doubt.”

Doubt is an advanced feature of reasoning. To be a good thinker, you need some degree of doubt. You need to question things and scrutinize whatever is put in front of you. That is one of the reasons why doubt is incorporated into these models.

Another reason is to avoid hallucinations. It is true that someone who does not doubt can be easily deluded. The same is true for LLMs. If a model does not question its own conclusions, it might hallucinate heavily, believing that it has solved a particular problem when it actually has not.

But it turns out that some problems are not bottlenecked by intelligence. They are bottlenecked by the willingness to continue, to be almost foolishly persistent about the problem and keep trying even when you are uncertain.

I noticed this while working on difficult problems myself. Sometimes, a less advanced model can get further than a more advanced model simply because it is more willing to pursue an idea, test it, fail, and try again. An overly cautious model can become trapped in its own doubt.

There have even been reports of Anthropic employees repeatedly telling Claude, “You can do it,” while working on difficult tasks. Whether or not the phrase itself is important, the underlying idea is interesting: sometimes a model needs encouragement to continue exploring rather than continually questioning whether its approach is valid.

Can we add or remove doubt from LLMs depending on the problem?

Not exactly, not as simply as turning a single “doubt” switch on or off. But we could potentially treat doubt as a control knob.

A model could have an exploration mode, where it inhibits doubt and is encouraged to pursue unconventional hypotheses, take temporary risks, and keep experimenting.

Then it could switch into a verification mode, where it increases skepticism and aggressively scrutinizes its own conclusions, searches for contradictions, and tries to falsify what it believes it has discovered.

In principle, the model could switch between these modes depending on the problem or the instructions it receives.

Perhaps the ideal reasoning system is therefore not one that has more doubt or less doubt, but one that knows when doubt is useful and when doubt is getting in the way.

The goal would not be to make the model blindly confident.

It would be to make it capable of saying:

“I might be wrong. But this is cheap to test, so let me try.”

And once it finds something promising:

“Now that I think I’ve solved it, let me assume I’m wrong and try to break my own solution.”

That combination of persistent exploration and ruthless verification may be more important for solving genuinely hard problems than intelligence alone.
