On Books Having No Ads

20 October 2026

It is often said by book enthusiasts that they love books because they contain no advertisements. I agree that books generally have no ads, but I think they might contain something potentially much more dangerous.

Ad, especially modern ones, rarely attempt to transform the way we understand the world. More often, they try to persuade us to buy a particular product. That, in itself, does not seem particularly dangerous. The product is usually the point.

Books, however, can change the way we think about the world. And the way we think influences far more than what we buy. It influences what we consider desirable, what we value, and the kind of lives we aspire to live.

Consider the Bible and the Quran. For centuries, these books have profoundly influenced how people understand morality, community, family, and the purpose of life. Their influence extends far beyond the act of reading. They help shape entire ways of living.

Now, think about what happens when a book persuades you to adopt a particular philosophy of life. You might not simply buy one product. You might buy into an entire ecosystem of products.

Consider exercise. Now more than ever, it is worth discussing the importance of physical activity. I exercise occasionally myself, and I have found myself embracing the principles of healthy living. But take a moment to examine the enormous number of products marketed under the banner of this so called healthy lifestyle. Suddenly, exercising is no longer just about moving your body. It can become about buying supplements, purchasing specialised equipment, wearing particular brands, following specific diets, and acquiring products that promise to optimise every aspect of your health.

The underlying idea might be good. The commercial ecosystem built around it is another matter.

Investing provides another example. Investing is a worthwhile pursuit, and I look forward to making investments myself in the near future. But consider how many people have been persuaded to invest their money after reading books about investing. Some books genuinely educate their readers and others might promote particular philosophies, strategies, or financial products. A reader can finish a book believing not merely that investing is important, but that they must adopt a particular investment philosophy, use certain services, or pursue an entire lifestyle organised around accumulating wealth.

Of course, none of this means that books are inherently bad, or that their influence is necessarily malicious. A book can liberate us from ignorance, challenge our assumptions, and help us live better lives. That is precisely what makes its influence so powerful.

An advertisement asks for your attention. A book can reshape the person who gives that attention.

And once your worldview has changed, you may begin making purchases that no individual advertisement could ever have persuaded you to make.

My point is not that all books are bad for your health. It is that not all books are good for it, either.

We should be just as critical of the ideas we consume as we are of the products we buy. After all, a product might occupy a place in our homes, but an idea can occupy a place in our minds. And sometimes, the most expensive thing a book sells us is not something we buy with money, but a way of seeing the world that we never think to question.
