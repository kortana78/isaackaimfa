Ways of Doing Mathematics

26 June 2026

There are several ways of doing mathematics. I have never really been a mathematician myself, but from what I have read and watched, I have come to understand this distinction.

The first way of doing mathematics is by directly attacking a problem. A problem such as the Goldbach conjecture can be approached head-on, with mathematicians attempting to construct a proof using existing tools and methods. A mathematician like Paul Erdős exemplified this style of work, focusing on solving specific problems through deep but direct reasoning.

The second way of doing mathematics is by developing theories. This approach is more like placing an ice block into boiling water: first, one develops the theory (the boiling water), and then later applies it to problems (the ice block). In this style, it is often not immediately clear whether the theory will solve a particular problem or even which problems it will eventually address. It is more about constructing frameworks, exploring consequences, and sometimes generating conjectures and theorems for their intrinsic structure and beauty. A mathematician like Alexander Grothendieck is often associated with this way of thinking.

The first approach can sometimes solve a specific problem directly, while the second approach may either create entirely new fields or reveal many previously hidden unsolved problems. The effectiveness of the first often depends on developments in the second. Some mathematicians are able to do both: they create new tools while also applying them to solve concrete problems.

The question then becomes: what kinds of tools should be created in order to address particular problems?

Personally, I find myself more aligned with the creation of theories than with direct problem-solving, although I do engage in direct problem-solving from time to time. Over time, I have developed a certain dislike for computation-heavy mathematics. Instead, I am more interested in understanding the underlying structure of computation itself and in developing frameworks that can absorb and reinterpret such problems within broader theoretical systems.

I do not want to focus on results like (1 + 1 = 2). Rather, I want to ask why a particular formal system allows such results in the first place.
